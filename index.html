AI4Y?

Rethinking the values and societal implications of AI research

AI4X builds machine learning to reach a goal in some domain. AI4Y? asks where the goal came from. A full-day, in-person workshop bringing ICLR researchers together with scholars in the humanities, arts, and social sciences.
29 or 30 April 2027, ICLR 2027, California, USA.

Why this, and not something else?

In AI4X research, machine learning methods are developed to navigate challenges and achieve outcomes in specific scientific domains. AI4Y? looks one step earlier in the research process. It asks why particular motivations are pursued as technical problems, why certain technical choices are made, and why some outcomes are treated as desirable or prioritized over others.

AI research often answers these questions implicitly. Deciding how work should be split between humans and AI presupposes a view of which roles suit each. Designing tasks around successful completion can lead models to pursue that objective beyond the intended environment. Trading helpfulness against safety in RLHF, choosing among human-specified rewards, or balancing neutrality against substantive engagement in preference data all make consequential judgments about what matters most.

Scholars in the humanities, arts, and social sciences (HASS) make those judgments explicit. They have shown how technologies are embedded in social and cultural systems rather than simply meeting pre-existing needs, how designs can embody forms of power and authority, and how users keep renegotiating what a system is for.

AI4Y? brings these communities into one conversation. Technical AI can formalize values and turn them into interventions; HASS can surface the motivations, priorities, and consequences those formalizations carry. The workshop complements existing work on AI ethics, safety, alignment, and responsible AI by focusing on the stage where research problems are first defined.

Call for papers

We invite novel and ongoing technical, empirical, and conceptual research on why particular AI research problems are pursued, how motivations and values shape technical choices, and what consequences those choices produce. Submissions from technical AI and from HASS are equally welcome, and each is reviewed by the standards of its own field.

From motivation to technical problem

Concepts such as autonomy, distributive justice, meaningful work, pluralism, or human flourishing don't arrive as ready-made machine learning objectives. Someone has to decide how they are represented through objectives, constraints, preference distributions, datasets, benchmarks, or algorithms.

What is lost when a socially desired outcome is formalized? Whose preferences are represented? How should contested or plural values be encoded when there is no single agreed target?

Example topics
  • Agentic cooperation, coordination, collective behaviour, and social choice
  • Resource allocation, welfare, and preference aggregation
  • Human–AI collaboration, delegation, and preservation of human agency
  • Trustworthy, safe, reliable, and controllable AI systems
  • Pluralistic or value-aware alignment
  • New objectives, benchmarks, algorithms, or theory motivated by desired societal outcomes
  • Conceptual analyses of values such as autonomy, fairness, meaningful work, or flourishing, and what is gained or lost when they are operationalized
  • Formalizing such values as technical objectives or constraints
  • Participatory, deliberative, or qualitative methods for eliciting the outcomes affected communities want
  • Historical and sociological studies of how research problems, benchmarks, or subfields came to be defined

From technical choice to consequences

Technical research can establish what objectives, datasets, architectures, algorithms, and evaluations actually produce. HASS offers empirical and normative perspectives on what those effects mean socially, legally, and institutionally.

How do objectives, datasets, or metrics shape what AI systems do? What assumptions do these choices encode? How can evidence of unintended consequences inform redesign?

Example topics
  • Objectives, loss functions, reward models, and optimization procedures that privilege particular behaviours, especially feedback-based and alignment optimization
  • Biases or unintended preferences in datasets, annotations, or human feedback
  • Benchmarks and evaluations that reward undesirable or incomplete notions of progress
  • Architectural or system-design choices that affect autonomy, participation, access, or control
  • Empirical analyses of unintended consequences or hidden assumptions in ML formulations
  • Alternative objectives, datasets, benchmarks, metrics, or designs that better reflect intended outcomes
  • Ethnographic, interview, or labour studies of the people who produce data, annotations, and feedback
  • Legal, regulatory, and institutional analyses of design choices for accountability, liability, competition, or rights
  • Critical and philosophical analyses of assumptions in widely used objectives, benchmarks, or evaluation practices
  • How AI systems are taken up, resisted, or repurposed compared with their designed purposes

Trade-offs between desired outcomes

Observed consequences may show that several desirable outcomes can't all be realized at once. Technical researchers bring multi-objective optimization, Pareto frontiers, constrained optimization, preference learning, and social choice to characterize these trade-offs.

Which outcomes should be traded off at all? Are they commensurable? Who bears the costs, and who should choose the operating point?

Example topics
  • Safety–helpfulness and robustness–utility trade-offs
  • Personalization–pluralism and individual–collective welfare
  • Automation–human agency and performance–human learning
  • Efficiency–fairness and privacy–utility
  • Multi-objective optimization, Pareto methods, constrained learning, and preference learning
  • Methods for identifying, measuring, or navigating competing objectives
  • When trade-offs are fundamental and when better formulations or design can reduce them
  • Normative analyses of which outcomes can legitimately be compared or traded
  • How technical choices distribute benefits, costs, and decision-making authority across stakeholders
  • Legal and policy frameworks for adjudicating competing values, such as proportionality and balancing tests

Format

Tiny or short papers of 2–4 pages, excluding references and appendices.

Technical tiny papers can report late-breaking experiments, self-contained theoretical results, re-analyses of published work, or fresh perspectives on existing papers. HASS tiny papers can present a single urgent finding, a direct reply to a recent technical paper, or a short communication on trends in AI venues.

Category

Every submission declares a primary category, Technical or HASS, which sets the methodological standards used in review. Interdisciplinary work should pick the category that best reflects its main contribution.

HASS submissions may follow their field's conventions (author–date citations, footnotes, essay form). Conceptual, qualitative, legal, historical, and critical work is welcome; new empirical results are not required.

Archival status

The workshop is non-archival. Accepted papers remain publicly available on OpenReview and on this website, and you are free to publish them elsewhere.

Use of LLMs

Follow the ICLR 2027 LLM usage policy. Short papers must disclose substantive LLM use in an acknowledgment or appendix. Submissions whose central argument, analysis, or results were generated by an LLM will be desk rejected, and AI-generated tiny papers are not permitted. Papers that study LLMs are welcome: this policy is about authorship, not subject matter.

Positioning statement

Every submission opens with a positioning statement of at most 100 words, placed before the abstract. It should name (i) the value, motivation, or desired outcome at stake; (ii) how the paper examines the link between motivations, technical formulations, and observed consequences or trade-offs; and (iii) an unresolved societal question that would benefit from cross-disciplinary discussion. Submissions without one will be desk rejected.

Not enough: "This paper is relevant because it studies the societal implications of LLMs." It names no value, no research choice, and nothing gained or lost.

How papers are reviewed

Technical and HASS papers share one rubric so they are valued equally, but each is judged by the conventions of its own tradition. Conceptual and position papers aren't expected to include new empirical results; they should make a clear, well-supported argument with concrete implications for AI research. Reviewers nominate papers for a Best Paper Award in each category.

Programme

Six invited talks, a moderated panel, two poster sessions, and spotlight presentations that pair technical and HASS papers so each community responds directly to the other's work.

Spotlight pairs

Each pair joins one technical paper with one HASS paper across two consecutive 20-minute sessions. One author presents for 10 minutes, the paired researcher responds with how their field reads the work, and open discussion follows. Then the roles reverse. Pairs are told at acceptance so partners can read each other's papers in advance.

Taking part

Travel support

We are seeking non-industry funding for student travel grants and registration waivers, prioritizing early-career and underrepresented researchers. Application details will be posted here.

Joining remotely

The workshop is in person, but accepted papers, posters, slides, and short recorded summaries will be on OpenReview and this site. Contributed talks can be given remotely if authors can't travel, and an organizer will relay online questions during live sessions. Talks will be recorded and shared with speakers' consent.

The AI4Y? Research Agenda

After the workshop we will publish a public research agenda drawing on accepted papers, the panel, and the spotlight pairs. It will trace motivations to formulations, implicit priorities, observed consequences, and open questions, and live on this site as a resource the community can return to.
